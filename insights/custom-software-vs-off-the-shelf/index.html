Custom software vs. off-the-shelf: which is right for a small business?

Short answer: buy off-the-shelf when a generic tool already does what you need, and go custom when you are stitching two or three generic tools together with your own time. Most small businesses do not pick one and stay there. They run off-the-shelf for the basics (email, accounting, scheduling) and build custom for the one process that is actually unique to how they operate.

I get this question in almost every first conversation, usually phrased as "can't I just buy something for this?" Sometimes yes. Here is how I actually think about it.

The real cost of off-the-shelf

A single SaaS subscription looks cheap: $50 to $200 a month is normal. The problem shows up when you need three or four of them to cover one workflow, and none of them share data. You end up doing the integration yourself, by hand, every day: exporting a report from one tool, reformatting it, and typing it into another. That labor is the real cost, and it does not show up on the software invoice. I have seen small businesses paying $400 to $600 a month across five tools and still running the connective work manually.

The real cost of custom

A custom starter project is $1,000, fixed price, delivered in about two weeks. It automates exactly one process, the one that is actually costing you time, and it talks to whatever system you already use. It is not a full platform and it is not meant to replace your accounting software or your point of sale. It is meant to remove the one manual step that generic software was never built to handle, because generic software has to work for everyone and your business is not everyone.

A real example

A fresh market was building its weekly ad flyers by hand: pulling product lists and prices, laying them out in a generic design tool, and fighting the template every single week. Off-the-shelf design software was not wrong for the job, it just was not built for the job. I built a tablet app that turns their product list directly into a print-ready flyer. What took days now takes an afternoon. Nobody touches a design tool anymore.

How to actually decide

Ask whether the tool needs to work for every business or just for yours. If you are scheduling meetings, sending invoices, or managing email, buy off-the-shelf. Those are solved problems and reinventing them costs more than it saves. If you are describing a process and catching yourself saying "well, except we also have to," that "except" is where custom pays off. It usually means you have been paying someone's salary, or your own time, to do by hand what software should be doing.

Is off-the-shelf software actually cheaper than custom?

Not always. A single subscription is cheaper than a custom build. But once you are running three or four tools to cover one workflow, and manually moving data between them every week, the labor cost of that manual work often exceeds a $1,000 starter project within a couple of months.

When does it make sense to build custom instead of buying software?

When the process is specific to how your business actually operates and no off-the-shelf tool fits it without you working around its limits. Signs it is time: you are exporting and re-entering the same data between two systems, or only one person in the company knows how to do a manual step correctly.
