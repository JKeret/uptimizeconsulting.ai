Is the $1,000 starter project a standalone deliverable, or do I have to buy a full platform afterwards?

It stands alone. The $1,000 starter project is a fixed price for one working automation, delivered in about two weeks, that you own and keep whether or not we ever work together again. I'm Jonathan at Uptimize Consulting, and there is no platform contract behind it, no subscription, and no second invoice waiting. Most clients do continue, but only because the first thing proved it was worth continuing.

The reason people ask this is that the pattern is familiar: a low entry price, a "discovery phase," and then the real proposal arrives. Agencies do it because a $1,000 project does not pay for an agency. It does pay for one person who scopes tightly and builds without meetings, which is what I do. So the starter is not a loss leader I need to recover on the next invoice. It is priced to be worth doing on its own.

What you get for $1,000, and what you do not

  • One process, chosen on a 30-minute call. The one that wastes the most of your week. I write down exactly what the app will do, and that scope is the contract.
  • Working software in about two weeks. Not a prototype, not a demo, not a "phase one deliverable." A tool your team uses on real work.
  • Everything in your name. Code in your repository, data in your accounts, hosting billed to you. If we never speak again, it keeps running.
  • Not included: a second process, integrations we did not agree on, or ongoing changes after handoff. Those are real work and they are priced separately, but only if you want them.

Why most clients continue anyway

A grocery retailer came to me with one job: their weekly ad flyers were built by hand, and it took days. The starter was a tablet app that turns a product list into print-ready promotional graphics. It shipped, the afternoon-instead-of-days result was obvious to everyone in the building, and only then did the conversation about "what else" happen. That is the whole design. The first project is small enough that you are not betting the business on a consultant you met three weeks ago, and real enough that the result speaks for itself.

When a client does continue, the next step is usually a second process or a platform in the $10,000 to $25,000 range, built in phases so you see working software after each one. Compliance-heavy or multi-system builds run $30,000 to $75,000 and up. Every one of those conversations starts from evidence, not from a pitch, which is why I would rather lose a platform sale than push one before the starter has earned it.

When it should stay a one-off

Plenty of businesses have exactly one process worth automating, and the honest outcome for them is a single starter and a handshake. Others prefer to run two or three separate $1,000 projects on unrelated problems over a year, and that works fine as long as those tools do not need to share data. A platform only makes sense when the processes need to talk to each other, when the same records flow through several steps, or when the pile of small tools has grown enough that maintaining them separately costs more than connecting them. If none of that is true, I will say so.

What about support after the two weeks

Care plans run $300 to $1,500 a month, scaling with how much of the business runs on the software. A single starter app sits at the bottom of that range, and it is optional. Some clients take it because they want someone on call when a vendor changes a file format. Others keep the app as-is and call me if something breaks. Both are fine, because the software is yours either way and any developer can maintain it. Nothing in the starter quietly depends on you buying care.

The one condition

The starter works because the scope is fixed on the call. If the process turns out to change every few weeks, or the data it needs lives only in someone's head, I will tell you on the call that it is not ready for a starter yet, and I would rather do that than take the $1,000 and deliver something you cannot use. That is the same honesty that makes the "no obligation afterwards" part real: I only want the next project if the first one deserved it.
