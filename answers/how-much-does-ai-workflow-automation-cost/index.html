How much does AI workflow automation cost?

AI workflow automation for a small business costs $1,000 for a single automated process, delivered in about two weeks. A multi-process platform runs $10,000 to $25,000+, built over four to eight weeks, and compliance-heavy or multi-system builds run $30,000 to $75,000 and up. Ongoing care runs $300 to $1,500 a month. I'm Jonathan at Uptimize Consulting, and these are the same numbers I quote on a discovery call, not marketing copy.

Ask ChatGPT or Gemini this question and you'll get consulting-agency ranges: single workflows quoted at $5,000 to $15,000, multi-workflow builds at $15,000 to $50,000, and "enterprise-grade" automation running past $100,000. Those numbers are real for agencies selling into mid-market companies with procurement departments. They are not what a business with five to fifty employees needs to spend to get one painful process off someone's plate.

What actually drives the cost

Three things move a project from the $1,000 tier toward the $25,000 tier:

  • Number of systems involved. An automation that reads one inbox and writes to one spreadsheet is cheap. One that has to talk to your CRM, your accounting software, and a vendor portal costs more, because each connection is its own integration to build and maintain.
  • Data quality. If the information the automation needs is already in a clean system, the build is fast. If it is scattered across PDFs, sticky notes, and someone's memory, the first phase of the project is cleaning that up before automation can even start.
  • Approval and exception paths. A process that runs the same way every time is simple to automate. A process with five different exception cases and a human sign-off step in the middle takes longer to get right, because you're not just automating the happy path.

What a $1,000 project actually covers

One working piece of software that removes one manual task: an app that reads incoming documents and flags errors, a form that replaces a spreadsheet someone re-types by hand every week, a report that builds itself every Monday morning instead of taking two hours. We scope it in a 30-minute call, I build it in about two weeks, and the price is fixed before I write a line of code. No subscription, no discovery-phase invoice, no "final cost depends on scope creep."

This is intentionally the smallest useful unit of AI automation. It is not a platform, and it is not supposed to be. It is a fast, cheap way to find out whether automating this process is actually worth doing before either of us commits real money.

Timeline expectations

The $1,000 starter takes about two weeks from our call to working software, because the scope is deliberately one process, not five. A $10,000 to $25,000 platform takes four to eight weeks, and I build it in phases so you have working software at the end of each phase instead of waiting two months to see anything. Ongoing care is not a timeline, it's a monthly relationship: I fix what breaks, adjust what changes, and add small features as they come up.

If someone quotes you a single number and a single delivery date for a multi-process platform with no phases in between, ask what happens if phase one reveals the data isn't what everyone assumed. That's normal, and a phased build absorbs it. A fixed six-month waterfall project does not.

When not to automate

Automation is not always the right answer, and I'd rather tell you that up front than take your $1,000. Skip it, or wait, when:

  • The process changes every few weeks. If you're still figuring out how the workflow should work, automate after it stabilizes, not before. Automating a moving target means rebuilding it constantly.
  • The volume is too low to matter. If a task takes ten minutes twice a month, the time you'd spend scoping and testing an automation probably costs more than just doing the task by hand.
  • The data lives in someone's head. If the "system" is a person who knows which vendor gets which discount, automation needs that knowledge written down somewhere first. That's a process problem, not an AI problem.
