What happens after a custom app project ends? Support, changes, and keeping it running.

You own the app, the code, and every account it runs on, and it keeps working whether or not I am involved. Most clients keep an optional care plan, $300 to $1,500 a month depending on how much software is under care, for monitoring, small changes, updates, and a person to call. I'm Jonathan at Uptimize Consulting, and I would rather you stay because the plan is useful than because you are stuck.

This is the question people are often too polite to ask on a first call, and it is the right one. Software that nobody looks after drifts: a login expires, a connected service changes its rules, a browser update breaks a button. The honest answer covers three things: what you own, what ongoing care costs, and where "small change" ends and "new project" begins.

What you own when the project ends

Everything. The code sits in a repository you control, the hosting account is in your company's name, and so are the API keys, the domain, and the database. I get access as a collaborator, not as the owner. If I disappeared tomorrow, another developer could pick up the handover notes and keep going. I set it up this way on purpose, because the alternative, where the consultant holds the keys, is how small businesses end up paying rent on their own software.

How the handover works

Every project ends with a short written handover, usually two to four pages, kept next to the code. It lists where the app runs, which accounts and keys it depends on, how to restart it, what to check when something looks off, and who to contact at each third-party service. For anything a staff member touches day to day, I record a five-minute screen walkthrough instead of writing a manual nobody reads. On the $1,000 starter, the handover is included in the price. On a platform build I also do a live session with whoever will own it internally.

The care plan: what it costs and what it covers

A care plan runs $300 to $1,500 a month, and the number scales with how much of your business runs on the software. A single starter app sits at the bottom of the range. A platform with several workflows, integrations, and a customer-facing portal sits at the top. In between, it grows as the software does. Every plan includes:

  • Monitoring, so I usually know something broke before you do.
  • Security and dependency updates, applied on a schedule rather than when something forces it.
  • Small changes: a new field, a changed rule, a tweaked report, anything that takes a few hours.
  • Fixes when a connected service changes, which is the single most common thing a plan pays for.
  • A person to call. Not a ticket queue, me.

A home-care agency I work with started with one app that reads claim statements. As that grew into a billing platform their whole office depends on, the plan grew with it, because the cost of an outage grew too. That is the logic behind the range.

Where a small change ends and a new project begins

The line is scope, not effort. Changing how an existing screen behaves is a small change. Adding a new screen, connecting a new system, or automating a workflow the app did not handle before is a new project, and I scope and price it separately, usually starting from the $1,000 starter. I tell you which side of the line a request falls on before doing it, so there are no surprise invoices. Most of what clients ask for in a normal month is on the small-change side.

Is the plan optional? Yes.

Most clients keep one, but nothing requires it. If you have someone technical in-house, or you prefer to call me only when something needs doing, that works. Per-request fixes cost more per hour than the plan does, and there is no monitoring, so problems surface when a staff member notices. For a starter app that runs once a week, that trade-off is often fine. For software your billing depends on, it usually is not.

What if I cancel the care plan?

Nothing changes on the day you cancel. The app keeps running, the code and accounts are already yours, and the handover notes still apply. What stops is the monitoring and the fast path for fixes. If something breaks six months later, you can bring me back on a per-project basis or hand the notes to any developer. Clients who pause during a slow season and come back later are common, and there is no penalty for it.

Who fixes it if a third-party API changes?

On a care plan, I do, and it is covered. Accounting platforms, CRMs, and messaging providers all change their APIs and rules, sometimes with a few weeks' notice, sometimes with none. Monitoring catches the failure, and I usually have it fixed before it affects a real workday. Without a plan, it is a small paid fix, typically a few hours of work, scheduled around current projects.

How fast do small changes happen?

On a care plan, most small changes ship within a few business days, and anything that stops work, a broken report on billing day, a form that will not submit, gets attention the same day. Without a plan, I fit changes around active projects, which usually means one to two weeks. Either way, I confirm the timeline when you ask, not after.
