Who owns the software after a custom app is built for my small business?

You own it. When I build a custom app for a small business, the code, the data, and the hosting accounts are all in your name from the first week, and you can hand the whole thing to any other developer whenever you like. I'm Jonathan at Uptimize Consulting, and I put that in writing because the most common fear I hear on a scoping call is "what happens if you get hit by a bus, or we fall out."

Ask ChatGPT or Gemini who owns custom software and you'll get a careful legal answer: under U.S. copyright law the developer owns the code by default unless the contract assigns it, so "check your work-for-hire and IP assignment clauses." That is accurate, and it is exactly why so many small businesses end up paying for software they cannot leave. My answer is simpler, because I settle it in the contract before I start: every project is an assignment of all rights to you, and the practical setup matches the paperwork.

What "you own it" means in practice

Ownership on paper is worth very little if the developer still holds the keys. So I set things up so the keys were never mine to begin with:

  • The code. It lives in a source repository under your organization's account. I work in it as a collaborator you can remove with one click. Every change I make is visible in the history.
  • The data. The database, the uploaded documents, the logs, all of it sits in accounts you own. If you want a full export tomorrow, you do not have to ask me, you run it yourself or have anyone else run it.
  • The accounts. Hosting, domain, email delivery, AI model access, whatever the app rents from outside. Opened in your name, billed to your card, with you as the administrator. I get an invitation, not ownership.
  • The documentation. A README in the repository that explains what the app does, how to run it, and which accounts it depends on. Written for the next developer, not for me.

A home-care agency I built for started with a $1,000 project that reads claim statements and flags billing errors. Before I wrote the first line, we opened the hosting and repository accounts under their business email. If they had decided after two weeks that they never wanted to hear from me again, they would have lost nothing: the app keeps running, the bill goes to them, and any developer can read the code.

How this differs from a SaaS subscription

With a subscription tool you own your data, in theory, and nothing else. The moment you stop paying, the software is gone, and "export your data" often means a CSV that no other system can read without a migration project. Pricing changes are not your decision, features get removed, and if the vendor gets acquired the product you chose may not exist in a year. None of that is a criticism of SaaS. For a standard need with a standard product, renting is the right call. The trade-off is that you never own the thing your business runs on.

A custom app flips that. There is no monthly license fee for the software itself, only the small hosting costs you pay to the provider directly. The features are the ones you asked for, and nobody can take them away.

How this differs from an agency that keeps the IP

This is the arrangement that surprises people most. Plenty of agencies build on a proprietary framework, host the finished product on their own infrastructure, and license it back to you. The contract says you "own" the application, but the layer it runs on is theirs, so leaving means rebuilding. Others assign you the code but keep the hosting, the domain, and the API keys, which is ownership without control. If you are reading a proposal now, look for three things: who holds the repository, whose name the hosting is in, and whether anything runs on a framework only that agency uses. If any answer is "them," you are renting.

What I keep, and why

I keep two things. First, my general know-how and any reusable building blocks that are not specific to your business, the same way a carpenter keeps their jigs. Those never contain your data or your business logic, and they are not what makes your app yours. Second, an invitation to your accounts for as long as we are working together, so I can fix and extend the software. Remove the invitation and I am out, with nothing of yours left in my hands. Care plans of $300 to $1,500 a month exist to keep the software working and growing, not to hold it hostage; you can cancel and keep everything.

When ownership matters less than you think

If the process you want automated is truly generic, such as sending appointment reminders or basic invoicing, owning the software is not worth a custom build. Buy the tool, accept the subscription, move on. Ownership starts to matter when the app encodes how your business actually works, when it holds records you cannot afford to lose access to, or when the monthly subscription math has quietly passed what a one-time build would have cost. That is the point where "who owns this" becomes the most important question in the proposal.
